<Lead>
When do humanoid robots beat human labour in warehouse economics? A discrete-event simulation
and Monte Carlo total-cost-of-ownership model on 2,359 real robot episodes and Austrian wage
rates. At the modelled demand, <strong>they do not yet</strong>.
</Lead>

The question is unit economics, not capability: at what price, demand and staffing does a
humanoid fleet become the cheaper way to run pick-move-place work in a warehouse? The model
prices seven staffing scenarios over five years at an 8% discount rate, from human-only crews
through AMR hybrids to pure-humanoid fleets.

<Marker num="01" label="Context" id="sec-01" />

The robot evidence is 2,359 episodes from five public UnifoLM datasets, pinned by hash. They are
household demonstrations (plates into a dishwasher, pillow pickup, clothes into a washing
machine, single- and dual-arm manipulation), not warehouse tasks. The model uses their
pick-move-place primitives as a proxy and applies a transfer factor of 0.50–0.90 to their speed.
That transfer is the largest threat to external validity, and it is stated before any result.

Labour is priced at Austrian collective-agreement (KV) wage rates; robot and AMR capex uses
public price assumptions. Integration cost and availability derating are part of the model.

<Marker num="02" label="Process" id="sec-02" />

Four modules run in order from one command:

1. **Capability extraction.** Cycle times and a rule-based task taxonomy from the episodes.
2. **Discrete-event simulation.** Seven scenarios × 15 replicas (105 runs), demand-bound at
   120 orders an hour, each scenario sized to a utilisation of at most 0.85.
3. **Five-year TCO with Monte Carlo.** 10,000 draws per scenario, one-at-a-time and Sobol
   sensitivity, and a binary search for the humanoid price at which the fleet breaks even.
4. **Reporting.** Executive charts, CSV exports, an executive summary in German and English,
   and a Tableau Public workbook.

The build runs under a CI-enforced governance protocol: every change closes a tracked finding
with a named verification script, and CI re-runs the script of every closed finding on each pull
request. One finding changed the headline. An earlier version compared fixed crews of
different sizes, eight people against six AMR-hybrid units, so its "cheapest option" mixed a
smaller crew with a technology choice. Every non-legacy scenario is now sized by the same
optimiser to minimum five-year cost under the utilisation cap, and the eight-person crew stays
only as a labelled reference for the cost of overstaffing.

The model's recommendation is to deploy the lean hybrid now and defer humanoid procurement. The
breakeven search ran humanoid capex from €10,000 to €500,000 per unit and found no price at
which a pure-humanoid fleet matches the lean hybrid's cost per order at 120 orders an hour. Even
near-free humanoids lose, so capex is not the binding constraint. The call is worth revisiting
under demand growth, labour scarcity or multi-shift operation.

For the leading scenario, the Monte Carlo cost has a mean of €461,414 with a standard deviation of
€43,755, and a 90% interval from €394,658 to €537,549.

One assumption cuts against the robots. The model assumes labour is available at KV rates. In
Austrian intralogistics the practical driver of automation is unfilled vacancies rather than
wage arbitrage, so robot value is understated wherever vacancies stay open.

What this does not establish:

- A site-specific procurement case. There are no warehouse-native cycle times and no signed
  humanoid quotes.
- Warehouse performance. Household demonstrations remain a proxy for warehouse tasks.
- Local prices. Public list prices and estimated KV wages can move the cost either way.
- Independent reproduction. The committed reproduction log was produced by an agent, not by a
  third party cloning the repository cold.
