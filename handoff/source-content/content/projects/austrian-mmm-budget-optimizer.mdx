<Lead>
A Bayesian marketing-mix model that estimates what each channel adds, tests platform-reported
attribution against a known truth, and moves budget only where the evidence clears a
breakeven rule.
</Lead>

The question is the one every advertiser asks of its media plan: which channels contributed
incrementally, how far off were the platform-reported numbers, and what does moving the same
budget actually gain? The working measure is contribution profit, not revenue. A channel that
returns less than its breakeven return on ad spend destroys margin, however large its reported
revenue looks.

<Marker num="01" label="Context" id="sec-01" />

"Austrian" names the setting the model is built for, not the data behind it: weekly grain,
the Austrian public-holiday calendar as a control, euro and contribution-margin logic. No
Austrian client data was used. The model runs in three layers, and the first has to pass
before the second counts.

- **Layer P**, a generated advertiser with disclosed true parameters: 156 weeks, five channels
  (TV, radio, print, paid search, paid social).
- **Layer R**, Meta's public Robyn demo file (MIT licence): simulated weekly data for five named
  channels, revenue in the file's own money units, competitor sales as the control.
- **Layer D**, the Layer R posterior handed to a budget optimiser under a written decision rule.

<Marker num="02" label="Process" id="sec-02" />

Each channel gets geometric adstock, so this week's spend keeps working at an estimated decay,
and a Hill saturation curve on the adstocked spend, so returns diminish as spend grows. A linear
trend, two yearly Fourier pairs and one control complete the baseline. Priors are weakly
informative and identical across channels, so the estimates come from the data rather than
from a prior that already knows the answer.

The model is fitted in PyMC and sampled with NUTS (nutpie): two chains of 1,000 tuning steps and
1,000 draws, target acceptance 0.9, raised to 0.99 by a recorded ladder after divergences. A fit
counts only with R-hat below 1.01, effective sample size above 400 and zero divergences, and
every fit writes those diagnostics next to itself.

Validation comes before any decision. On Layer P the sampler has to recover a truth it was
handed, and the platform-style attribution is scored against the same truth:

The misses follow a known trade-off rather than a bug. All five effect sizes and all five
half-saturation points have medians above the truth, because a curve that rises higher but
saturates later fits the same weeks. Recovery is therefore judged on curves and shares, which
are what a budget decision uses. The platform-style report, meanwhile, gives the offline
channels no credit at all.

The decision rule is fixed before the optimiser runs: shift budget toward a channel only while
the lower bound of its marginal-ROAS 90% interval stays above breakeven, and only if the 10th
percentile of the reallocation gain is positive. At an assumed contribution margin of 40%,
breakeven ROAS is 2.50.

<Marker num="03" label="Results" id="sec-03" />

On the public dataset, the same total weekly budget reallocated under that rule gains a median
15.1% of current media contribution. The 10th percentile is 8.8%, and none of the 500 posterior
draws used shows a loss.

| Channel | Weekly spend now | Recommended | Marginal ROAS now (90% interval) |
|---|---|---|---|
| TV | 14,844 | 22,266 (+50%) | 7.26 (4.38–10.72) |
| Out-of-home | 43,218 | 34,723 (−20%) | 0.75 (0.10–1.68) |
| Print | 3,729 | held | 7.81 (1.22–16.01) |
| Facebook | 2,146 | 3,218 (+50%) | 20.53 (4.76–45.52) |
| Search | 5,916 | held | 7.68 (0.89–25.55) |

The breakeven gate is what holds print and search. Their medians are high, but the lower bounds
of their intervals fall below 2.50, so no budget moves toward them. Amounts are in the dataset's
own money units, not euros; shares, ROAS ratios and the breakeven test are the quantities that
carry over.

On a 26-week holdout against two baselines, the model had the lowest error on Layer P (MAPE 3.0%,
against 4.7% for ridge regression and 9.6% for a seasonal naive forecast) and lost on Layer R
(7.9% against ridge regression's 5.1%). That is reported as it came out. The case for the MMM is
that it says which channel earned the revenue, with intervals, which the baselines cannot. On
Layer P its 90% intervals covered 85% of holdout weeks: slightly too narrow, so the model is a
little overconfident.

What this does not establish:

- Layer R is a simulated public dataset in unnamed money units. A swap-in of real advertiser
  data is planned, not done.
- One model form is assumed (geometric adstock, Hill saturation, additive baseline). Recovery on
  Layer P shows the sampler recovers that form, not that reality has it.
- Weekly national data cannot separate channels whose spend moves together; the intervals widen
  accordingly.
- The optimiser assumes response curves hold at new spend levels and that competitors do not
  react.

Complete, last validated 2026-09-16. The numbers in the repository README are rendered from
the report files, and a test checks that the two agree. Code, reports and the decision memo:
