<Lead>
An evidence base for data-career decisions in Austria, with a Styria and Graz focus: a one-day
snapshot of 720 core data-role postings from five public sources, the AMS JobBarometer
2020–2025 and Eurostat vacancy seasonality.
</Lead>

The questions are practical. Which data titles are advertised in Austria, where, and with which
technologies, languages, education, pay and work model? How does Styria differ from Vienna? The
standard set for the answers: a technically competent, sceptical reader should be able to audit
the method, the provenance, the limitations and the conclusions.

<Marker num="01" label="Context" id="sec-01" />

Postings were collected on 2026-09-16 between 17:00 and 18:40 UTC from EURES, karriere.at,
LinkedIn's logged-out job pages, willhaben and jobs.at, using 45 title keywords in English and
German, with no login and no CAPTCHA or paywall bypass. StepStone, Indeed, hokify and Glassdoor
were blocked and are excluded. The official series are the AMS JobBarometer (online ads per
occupation class and federal state, 2020–2025) and Eurostat's quarterly job-vacancy data.

A terms-of-use audit found that all five posting sources restrict automated extraction. The
collection therefore stays a one-off private research set. The collectors and the raw ads are
not published; the public repository holds the code, the rules and the aggregated tables, with
every text and URL column removed.

<Marker num="02" label="Process" id="sec-02" />

Every step is a script and every rule lives in a config file. Titles are normalised by ordered
regex rules into eight role families. Geography comes from explicit fields, then NUTS-3 codes,
then postcodes and place names in the text. Salary figures are parsed from structured fields or
from text near salary words and annualised at 14 monthly payments. Language requirements are
read from ±120-character windows around each language mention. Duplicates are grouped by
union-find over company, title, federal state and a description fingerprint.

Text-derived shares use postings with a description longer than 300 characters as the
denominator, every table stores its n, and proportions that feed a decision carry Wilson 95%
intervals. The official series are analysed as their own units and never merged with
posting-level statistics. A 67-test suite checks rule behaviour and output consistency, and a
digest script reprints every number quoted in the documents from the tables.

<Marker num="03" label="Results" id="sec-03" />

- Vienna holds 48% of open core postings. Styria is 7.5% of the snapshot but 13.6% of the
  official yearly flow, and the only large region above its 2020 level (AMS "Data Scientist"
  class: Austria −28%, Styria +34%).
- Data engineering and data science are the largest families, at 22% each. Marketing- and
  product-analytics titles are rare: 25 of 720, none in Styria.
- SQL (40%) and Python (35%) lead, and the stack is Microsoft-centred: Azure 19%, Power BI 18%,
  Tableau 3%.
- 40% of ads state a German requirement. 24% are written in English, but 19% of those still
  state one.
- 81% of advertised salary figures are collective-agreement minimums, so the ads show floors,
  not pay.
- Seasonality is small against the cycle: within-year amplitude of 5–14% in the Eurostat series,
  against 255–567% between years.

The findings feed two decision documents that score each role family against a declared skill
profile, with stated weights and a sensitivity check under alternative weights. There is no
hidden scoring, and each statement traces to a table id.

What this does not establish:

- Demand over time. It is a one-day stock of ads, not yearly flow, vacancies or hires.
- Full coverage. StepStone, Indeed and company career pages are missing, and title-based
  inclusion has measured precision but unmeasured recall.
- Styrian detail. Styrian cells are small (n = 58), so they are reported as counts and labelled
  tentative.
- Anything causal. There are no hiring outcomes, so no requirement is evidence of what gets
  someone hired.
